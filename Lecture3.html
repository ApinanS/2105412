3-1 · stop rule

Two jobs, then stop digging

Three layers named. Now the practical question: how far down must you go before you are allowed to act?

Stop rule Dig until it predicts a number and names what to change
  • Too shallow: “the exchanger is fouling” — predicts nothing, changes nothing
  • Deep enough: “3 mm deposit halves U, outlet falls 14 °C, shell-side ΔP unchanged”
  • Too deep: radical polymerisation, dissolved oxygen — Stage 6

3-2.2 · layer 2 of 5 · equipment behaviour

Can this hardware even do what you accuse it of?

Proposed causeClaimed symptomWhat the hardware actually does
tube-side fouling in a shell-and-tube exchanger“…so the tube ΔP fell”
  • Impossible
  • Deposits narrow the flow area, so ΔP must rise at the same flow
  • ΔP fell → lower flow, or a leak bypassing the tubes
a centrifugal pump “worn out”“…so the discharge pressure dropped, but the flow rate is unchanged”
  • Fights the head–capacity curve
  • On one curve, lower head at the same flow is not a point the machine can sit on
a control valve stuck at 40 %“…but the flow reading still moves all the time”
  • Possible, but only if something else moves
  • Upstream pressure, or a parallel line
  • A systems-thinking clue — section 3-4
positive-displacement pump delivers less“…because the discharge line is plugged”
  • A PD pump delivers volume set by geometry and speed
  • A blockage drives pressure up until something relieves or breaks
  • It does not cut the flow rate

Weeks 7–10 pay off here. Today, practise asking the question well

Five layers, one paragraph

The paragraph “Steam +12 % after turnaround. Fouling cut reboiler ΔP 0.8 to 0.5 bar. Efficiency 96 %, was 71 %; only worry: utility bill”

Find a flaw in Layers 1, 2, 3; name the single measurement to ask first (10 marks)

USD 50 walk-and-check, not a USD 3,700 lab sample

  • Layer 1: efficiency leaping 71 → 96 % across a turnaround is a changed definition — overall versus Murphree — not a real gain

  • Bonus layer 4: more steam, better separation, lower ΔP — energy is leaving unrecorded: suspect a blowing trap or leaking bypass
  • First measurement: walk out and check the reboiler condensate trap and bypass valve. USD 50, one operator, no shutdown

3-3.1 · tool 1 of 4 · mass balance

Every diagnosis starts by closing a box

$$\underbrace{\frac{dm}{dt}}_{\text{accumulation}} = \sum \dot m_{in} - \sum \dot m_{out} + \dot m_{gen} - \dot m_{cons}$$

Steady state: left-hand term zero — the gap is information, not noise

  • Close one unit first, then the whole plant
  • By component, not just total — a total closes while a component screams
  • Level and inventory are the accumulation term: falling level, not steady

What a gap means

GapRead it as
In > out
  • Accumulating inside
  • Leaking to atmosphere or to ground
  • Outlet meter reads low
Out > in
  • Inventory drawn down
  • Unmeasured feed — utility water, purge
  • Inlet meter reads low
Total closes, components do not
  • A reaction is happening
  • Sample point unrepresentative
  • Analyser out of calibration

3-3.3 · tool 3 of 4 · phase equilibrium

Temperature–pressure–composition triple allowed?

$$y_i = K_i\,x_i \qquad \sum K_i x_i = 1 \;\text{at the bubble point}$$

$x_i,\,y_i$ liquid / vapour mole fraction (–) · $K_i$ equilibrium ratio at measured $T$, $P$ (–)

  • Tray below bubble point: composition, pressure or thermocouple wrong
  • Reboiler outlet above dew point: superheating, not boiling
  • On or between the T–x–y curves, never outside

Three plant questions

  • Vapour at pump suction? Suction pressure vs bubble point pressure
  • Total reflux? Top/bottom temperatures vs pure-component boiling points
  • Stream that pure? Relative volatility vs available stages

3-3.4 · tool 4 of 4 · hydraulics

Pressure drop is a flow meter you own

$$\Delta P = K \rho Q^{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\Delta P_2}{\Delta P_1} = \left(\frac{Q_2}{Q_1}\right)^{2}$$

$\Delta P$ pressure drop (bar) · $K$ fitting or bundle resistance (–) · $\rho$ density (kg/m³) · $Q$ volumetric flow (m³/s)

  • ΔP up, flow unchanged: fouling
  • ΔP down, flow unchanged: leak or bypass
  • ΔP and flow on the square law: cause is upstream

Level from ΔP

$$h = \frac{\Delta P}{\rho g}$$

$h$ liquid head (m) · $g = 9.81$ m/s²

DP cell measures head, not level — foam: level wrong, transmitter healthy

3-4.1 · how faults move

3-4.2 · path 1 — recycle

Recycle makes disturbances large and slow

$$\text{steady-state gain} = \frac{1}{1-R}$$

R = fraction returned; at 0.8, fivefold

  • Time constant stretches too — slow settling
  • No exit — impurity builds until purge or reaction
  • Recycle carries heat too — feed–effluent exchanger

What “steady-state gain” means

How much bigger the settled effect is than the upset that started it. Gain 1 = no amplification.

The disturbance goes round again each pass: $1 + R + R^{2} + \dots = \frac{1}{1-R}$. At $R = 0.8$ that is 5×.

Signature

  • Step response ≫ design calculation
  • Drift with steps — operator corrections returning

Case 27 · the equipment

But the vapour is inside the tube — how does it get out uncondensed?

Because condensing inside a horizontal tube does not plug the tube. The condensate runs as a layer along the bottom while the vapour flows above it — a two-phase channel with an open vapour core right to the tube exit. Nothing the tube does to itself ever closes that core. The only thing that closes it is a liquid level imposed from the outlet side.

Case 27 · the equipment

The tubes are in parallel — so a bypass steals the flow

$$\frac{\rho_L}{\rho_v} = \frac{715}{2.0} \approx 350$$
  • IPA vapour at 88.4 °C, 1 atm: \(\rho_v = PM/RT \approx 2.0\) kg/m³. The same 1.252 kg/s is 618 L/s as vapour but only 1.75 L/s as liquid
  • Total area being sufficient means sufficient on average. Nothing forces each individual tube to condense its own share
  • A tube passing vapour has a far lower Δp than one running full of condensate, so it takes more flow — and then passes even more vapour. The maldistribution runs away
